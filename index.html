The Confederation Problem

It is 1786. Thirteen states, one vote each, and a Congress that cannot tax, cannot regulate trade, cannot enforce a law, and cannot change its own rules without every state agreeing. Students find out what that actually feels like.

What students are supposed to discover

Passing a law is not the same as anything happening

Congress votes to raise $1,000,000. Then every state privately decides whether to actually send its share — and nothing happens to the ones that refuse. Most classes collect about a third of what they voted for. The real Congress collected about a sixth.

One state out of thirteen can stop everything

Round 3 needs all thirteen states. Rhode Island will not agree, because in 1782 it did not agree. Students can spend the whole round trying to move it. Almost nobody ever does.

Playing well and losing anyway

Score rewards protecting your own state, so refusing to pay is genuinely the smart move. If enough states play smart, national stability collapses and every state — including the leader — loses twenty points.

The past has consequences

Round 4 is an emergency that has to be paid for out of the treasury Round 1 was supposed to fill. Classes that defected early find there is nothing left when they need it.

Everything runs in the browser. Nothing about a student is stored except the first names they type in.
